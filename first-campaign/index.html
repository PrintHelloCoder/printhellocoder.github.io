Create Your First Variable Printing Campaign

A step-by-step route from a campaign idea to an approved, measurable mailing.

Updated 14 October 2026 · 7 min read · 6 references

A first campaign should be simple enough to inspect and specific enough to teach you something. The objective is not to personalize every available field. It is to send a useful, accurate message to a defined audience and establish a production process you can repeat.

This guide uses a fictional business-service postcard as a running example. All quantities and decisions are illustrative. Adapt the format and approval steps to your own postal market, printer, data, and budget.

1 Write a one-page campaign brief

Start with the audience, the business reason to contact them, and the next action you want them to take. For the example campaign, imagine an equipment service company approaching independent workshops in a defined service area. A useful action might be requesting a maintenance assessment. A vague invitation to “learn more” makes both the creative and the measurement harder.

Specify who is excluded. Businesses outside the service area, existing opportunities already in active discussion, and people who should not receive marketing may need to be removed before the design work begins. Decide who owns those exclusions and when the list will be frozen.

Add an operational deadline and a financial boundary. Work backward from the desired arrival window, allowing time for proofs, corrections, printing, finishing, and postal handoff. Canada Post’s small-business guide is a useful Canadian planning reference; use the relevant postal operator for other countries. [2] Do not promise delivery dates based only on how quickly a printer can produce sheets.

2 Prepare a small, inspectable dataset

Create a record ID, company name, recipient name where known, mailing address, segment, and response destination. Separate address components into consistent fields. Keep only the information needed for the job and store the approved source where the production team can identify its version.

For a learning pilot, 100 or 200 records may be operationally manageable, but the right quantity depends on your economics and the outcome you want to measure. A small run can test the workflow without being large enough to establish a reliable response-rate difference. Do not confuse a successful production trial with proof of commercial performance.

Check duplicates using more than a single name field. Review companies that share addresses and recipients with multiple locations. Validate service-area eligibility independently of formatting. A perfectly formatted address can still be the wrong destination for your offer. Consult Canada Post’s addressing guidance when preparing Canadian mail. [3]

3 Choose meaningful variables

For the first version, use three or four variables at most: the company name, a relevant service line, an appropriate image, and a response code. Keep the main layout and offer stable. This limits the number of conditions you must proof and makes mistakes easier to trace.

Write a clear fallback for every optional field. A missing contact name might produce “Hello” without a name. An unavailable photograph might use an approved service image. A missing delivery address should hold the record. Make these decisions in writing before the merge, rather than improvising when the operator encounters the first error.

Use assets you have permission to reproduce, and ensure they support the message. A company logo is not evidence that the company endorses your offer. A photograph should not imply that a prospect is already your customer. The production checklist should include factual accuracy as well as image resolution.

4 Build the template around the finished object

Confirm the finished size and postal requirements before creating the layout. Reserve the delivery-address area and any required clear zones. Leave adequate safe space inside the trim edge, and supply bleed only as specified by the print provider. The address side is part of the design, not an empty area to fill at the end.

Arrange the front around one clear idea. Put supporting detail and the next step where they are easy to find. Use a short response URL in readable text even when there is a QR code. A recipient who cannot or does not want to scan should still have a way to respond.

Adobe’s Data Merge workflow is one option for connecting the template to your data. [1] Before composing the full job, create a test set that includes the longest company name, every segment, missing optional fields, and unusual image proportions. Keep a separate approval proof so later revisions cannot silently replace what the campaign owner reviewed.

5 Make the response path work

The landing page should continue the promise made on the card. If the card offers an assessment, the page should explain the assessment and how to request it. Avoid sending every recipient to a generic homepage that forces them to find the offer again.

Test the page on a phone using a printed proof. Check that the code scans under ordinary lighting, that the destination loads, and that the response form is usable. DENSO WAVE’s QR guidance explains why symbol dimensions and module size matter. [4] Decorative styling should not come at the expense of a readable code.

Use an opaque identifier for recipient-specific tracking instead of putting personal information directly in the URL. Decide who receives inquiries and how quickly they will respond. A well-tracked campaign can still fail if a form submission sits unnoticed in an inbox. Run a test inquiry and confirm the whole handoff, including any confirmation message.

6 Approve data, artwork, and production separately

Use three approval gates. The list owner approves who will receive the mailing. The campaign owner approves the message and design. The production owner approves the physical specification and the proof. One person may hold more than one role, but the decisions remain distinct.

At the data gate, reconcile imported, excluded, held, and eligible records. At the artwork gate, inspect every content branch and the known edge cases. At the production gate, inspect a finished sample: correct stock, correct sides, readable text, clean trimming, and a working response path.

Document any late change and repeat the checks it affects. Correcting a telephone number requires another content proof. Changing stock requires production evaluation. Replacing the mailing list requires another data reconciliation and potentially another merge. The earlier approvals should not be treated as permission for an unrelated version to proceed.

APPROVAL RECORD

Record the data version, template version, eligible count, proof filename, approver, and approval time. These identifiers make “approved” refer to a specific job.

7 Print, finish, and reconcile

Send the approved output with a concise job ticket. Include quantity, size, stock, sides, color expectations, finishing instructions, and the required sequence. Clarify whether the print provider is expected to impose the job. Keep the source data separate from the final print file so an operator does not accidentally regenerate it from a newer spreadsheet.

During production, track spoiled pieces and reprints. If the job is duplex, check orientation and front-to-back matching after any restart. If the job is cut from larger sheets, verify the finished order after trimming. Count completed pieces at the recipient-document level, not just the number of sheets fed through the machine.

Keep a small set of approved production samples and the final manifest. Record the actual postal handoff date. If a service provider handles mailing, request the agreed documentation of that handoff. The lettershop guide covers these responsibilities in more detail.

8 Measure the outcome and decide what comes next

Choose the primary outcome before mailing: qualified inquiries, held appointments, or customers acquired. Track scans and visits as diagnostic signals, but do not silently redefine success around whichever metric happens to look strongest. Include creative, data, print, postage, handling, and follow-up effort in the cost record.

Set a review date appropriate to the offer and buying cycle. A complex service may take longer to convert than a simple retail promotion. Record late responses rather than excluding them because the first reporting window ended. Where feasible, use a comparable holdout group to investigate whether the mailing added results beyond other activity.

At the review, ask two different questions: did the production process work, and did the campaign justify another test? You may have excellent print quality but an unconvincing offer, or strong interest with a cumbersome landing page. Printing Impressions’ strategy discussion is useful practical reading on connecting the audience and creative choices. [5]

For the second run, change a clearly defined element and preserve what you learned. Build a test log with the hypothesis, audience rules, version, spend, and outcome. That record is more useful than a folder of attractive postcard designs without any explanation of what happened after they were mailed.
